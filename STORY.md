# Lot 95

A short story

---

Priya Malhotra had the blotter memorized the way other people memorized bus routes. Tuesday through Sunday, incidents came in from the Stanford University Department of Public Safety in a spreadsheet nobody outside the paper ever saw, and by Wednesday night she turned that spreadsheet into six inches of type for the following week's crime section. Bike theft. Vehicle burglary. Petty theft from a building. The words repeated themselves so often that she had stopped hearing them, the way a nurse stops hearing a heart monitor.

It was late October, midterms bleeding into the second half of the quarter, when she noticed Lot 95 for the third time in one week.

*Burglary from a motor vehicle, between 11 a.m. and 12 p.m., reported at 295 Galvez Street, Lot 95 — Track House.*

Then, four lines down, the same address again, ten minutes earlier by the timestamp. Then a third, same lot, same narrow window, a different car. Three cars broken into within an hour, in a lot that belonged to the track and field facility, on a Saturday when the team was hosting an invitational.

She almost let it go. The blotter was full of small repetitions — bike racks stripped clean outside Wilbur, a run of catalytic converter thefts near the med school garages the previous spring, the kind of pattern that came from opportunity rather than intention. A meet meant visiting teams, rental vans, out-of-town plates, unfamiliar faces who wouldn't notice someone testing door handles. It was the obvious read. She wrote the item the obvious way and filed it.

What stopped her was the taxonomy.

The Daily's system tagged every blotter item with a category and a set of numeric codes before it ever reached her desk — theft, vehicle, campus-safety, the mechanical scaffolding underneath a story that readers never saw. Priya had built a habit, mostly out of boredom, of exporting those codes into a spreadsheet of her own, just to see if anything clustered in ways the prose didn't show. It was a habit her editor teased her about. It was also the only reason she caught it: every Lot 95 incident that fall carried the same secondary tag, a code reserved for thefts where the reporting party held a coaching or staff credential rather than a student one.

Three cars. Three coaches. Three schools that had all sent teams to Cobb Track for the same Saturday invitational.

She pulled the meet program from the athletics department archive, a PDF nobody had thought to protect, and matched it against the parking assignments SUDPS had logged in prior incident reports over two years — public record, technically, if you knew which request form to file and how long to wait. The visiting-team lot rotated by sport and by season, but Lot 95 came up for track meets with a regularity that had nothing to do with chance. Someone with a parking pass that let them loiter near Cobb without drawing attention had hit the same lot four times in eighteen months, always during a meet, always vehicles with out-of-county plates, always gone before the events finished and the coaches came back to their cars.

Priya sat with that for a long time before she did anything with it. She was twenty, she was not a detective, and the blotter had taught her exactly how far a theory could get you before it needed someone with a badge. What she had was a pattern legible only because she'd spent a year turning bureaucracy into a spreadsheet — not evidence, just the shape evidence might take.

She called the public information officer at SUDPS the way she'd been trained to, gave her name, gave the paper's name, and asked a question built to sound smaller than it was: whether the department tracked repeat locations for vehicle burglary same as it tracked repeat locations for anything else. The officer, a woman named Delgado who'd fielded three years of Priya's Wednesday-night questions without ever once losing patience, went quiet on the line in a way that told Priya she wasn't the first person to ask.

"We're aware of a pattern at that lot," Delgado said, which was as much as she was going to get, and which was also, Priya understood, a kind of confirmation. "I can't discuss an open investigation. You can quote me on 'aware.'"

The story that ran three weeks later didn't have Priya's spreadsheet in it, and it didn't have a name, because there wasn't an arrest yet and the paper didn't print theories. It had two sentences, tucked into a Thursday blotter roundup between a report of graffiti near the d.school and a stolen scooter outside Toyon: *SUDPS confirmed it is investigating a series of vehicle burglaries at Lot 95 connected to recent track and field meets. Anyone with information is asked to contact the department directly.* Six months after that, an arrest report crossed her desk with the flat, procedural language the blotter always used, no drama in it at all, a name Priya had never seen before attached to charges that matched, almost exactly, the shape she'd drawn on her own two years earlier.

She read it twice, then filed it the way she filed everything, in the queue for Wednesday's roundup, one more line among fifteen others. Bike theft. Vandalism. Grand theft, vehicle burglary, arrest made. By Thursday it would sit on the site same as it always did, unglamorous, uncredited, a single sentence between a residence hall noise complaint and a report from the GSB.

What stayed with her wasn't the case. It was how ordinary the discovery had felt from the inside — not a chase, not a confrontation, just a girl with a spreadsheet noticing that a lot's name kept appearing in a column most people never opened. Stanford printed itself, most days, as a place of enormous ambitions: cures, algorithms, the next thing. The blotter told a smaller, steadier story underneath that one, a campus of four-figure enrollments and six-figure grants where somebody's window still got smashed for a laptop charger, where a coach still came back from cheering on a fifteen-hundred-meter final to find her glovebox open and her registration gone. Priya had spent a year thinking the codes were the boring part of the job. It turned out the codes were the only part of the job that told the truth without trying to.

She closed the spreadsheet, opened a new tab, and started on next week's roundup.

*This story is a work of fiction. Its characters, dialogue, and central case are invented; the campus locations named are real, drawn from the format and geography of the crime reporting curated in the* Stanford Daily *police blotter dataset, but no event, arrest, or investigation described here corresponds to any actual incident on record.*
